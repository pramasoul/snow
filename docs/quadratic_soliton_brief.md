# Quadratic Solitons in PPLN — Physics Brief for CC

## Purpose

Build a notebook that demonstrates **temporal soliton formation mediated by
χ⁽²⁾ nonlinearity** in a PPLN waveguide, using SNOW's existing NEE solver.
No new physics terms are needed — quadratic solitons emerge naturally from the
coupled fundamental/SH dynamics that SNOW already models.

This is a different operating regime from the OPA notebooks. There, the goal
was energy transfer between waves (amplification). Here, the goal is stable
pulse propagation — the nonlinear coupling provides a self-phase-shift that
balances dispersion, locking the pulse into a shape-preserving bound state.

---

## Physical Picture

### The cascaded χ⁽²⁾ mechanism

Consider a pulse at frequency ω propagating through a PPLN crystal with
**large deliberate phase mismatch** Δk (i.e., the poling period is NOT
optimized for efficient SHG). What happens:

1. The χ⁽²⁾ interaction generates a small SH field at 2ω
2. Because Δk is large, the SH doesn't build up — it oscillates, converting
   back to the fundamental within a coherence length L_coh = π/Δk
3. The round-trip (FH → SH → FH) imprints a nonlinear phase shift on the
   fundamental that is proportional to intensity and inversely proportional
   to Δk:

       Δφ_NL ∝ d_eff² · I / (n² · Δk)

   This is mathematically identical to the Kerr effect (SPM), with an
   effective n₂:

       n₂_eff ∝ d_eff² / Δk

4. The sign of n₂_eff is determined by the sign of Δk:
   - Δk > 0 (poling period too short for perfect QPM): n₂_eff > 0
     (self-focusing, like silica)
   - Δk < 0 (poling period too long): n₂_eff < 0 (self-defocusing)

5. If n₂_eff > 0 and the GVD at ω is anomalous (β₂ < 0), or if n₂_eff < 0
   and GVD is normal (β₂ > 0), the SPM-GVD balance supports temporal
   soliton formation — the fundamental soliton of the nonlinear Schrödinger
   equation, but with the nonlinearity coming entirely from χ⁽²⁾ cascading.

### The quadratic soliton (simulton)

At moderate phase mismatch, the SH field is not negligible — it co-propagates
as a bound partner to the fundamental. The result is a two-color soliton:
a coupled FH + SH pulse pair that propagates together without dispersing.
This is the true "quadratic soliton" and it doesn't reduce to an effective
Kerr picture — both fields are dynamically active.

The notebook should explore both regimes: large Δk (cascaded, effectively
Kerr-like) and moderate Δk (genuinely quadratic, two-color bound state).

a bright soliton, or work with dark solitons, or shift to a wavelength

## PPLN Configuration

### Poling period: deliberately detuned

The key difference from the OPA notebooks: **we don't want phase matching.**
We want a controlled, large Δk.

Choose a poling period Λ_soliton that differs from the perfect-QPM period
Λ_QPM by enough to give a desired Δk:

    Δk = 2π/Λ_QPM − 2π/Λ_soliton

Or equivalently, use the QPM period from Tutorial 7 or the CW OPA and operate
at a temperature far from the phase-matching temperature, so that thermal
detuning provides the Δk.

**Target Δk range:** The relevant dimensionless parameter is Δk · L, where L
is the crystal/waveguide length. The regimes are:

| Δk · L        | Regime                                  |
|----------------|-----------------------------------------|
| 0              | Perfect phase matching — efficient SHG, no soliton |
| 1–10           | Strong coupling, quadratic soliton territory |
| 10–100         | Transitional — SH still non-negligible  |
| > 100          | Cascaded limit — effective Kerr, SH is slaved |

For a 20 mm waveguide, Δk · L = 100 means Δk = 5000 /m = 50 /cm, which is
a poling period detuning of only ~0.1 µm from Λ_QPM. For Δk · L = 10, it's
Δk = 500 /m, an even smaller detuning. These are very fine adjustments — in
practice, temperature tuning away from the QPM temperature by a few tens of
degrees may be the easiest way to dial Δk.

**Practical approach:** Fix the poling period at whatever SNOW already has
(5.18 µm from Tutorial 7, or 5.0 µm from the CW OPA), and use temperature
as the detuning knob. Compute Δk(T) from SNOW's temperature-dependent
Sellmeier model, and choose temperatures that place you in the cascaded
and quadratic regimes.

## Pulse Parameters

### Fundamental pulse

- **Duration:** 100–500 fs FWHM sech² (solitons are sech-shaped, so start
  with the right family)
- **Energy:** This is what we need to find — the soliton energy. For a
  conventional (Kerr) fundamental soliton:

      E_sol = |β₂| / (γ_eff · T₀)

  where γ_eff = n₂_eff · ω / (c · A_eff) is the effective Kerr nonlinearity
  from cascading and T₀ is the pulse duration parameter (T_FWHM ≈ 1.76 T₀
  for sech²).

  For TFLN with d_eff ≈ 17 pm/V and moderate Δk, the effective n₂ can be
  very large — n₂_eff ~ 10⁻¹⁷ to 10⁻¹⁵ m²/W depending on Δk, compared to
  silica's 2.6×10⁻²⁰ m²/W. This means soliton energies will be
  correspondingly lower (weaker pulses suffice).

  **Estimate for CC to verify:** For |β₂| ≈ 100 fs²/mm (typical for TFLN
  near 1550 nm), γ_eff ≈ 10 /W/m (from cascading at moderate Δk),
  T₀ = 100 fs:

      P_sol = |β₂| / (γ_eff · T₀²) ≈ 10⁻²⁵ / (10 · 10⁻²⁶) = 1 W

  So soliton peak power of ~1 W, energy ~100 fJ. Very modest — well within
  the range of fiber oscillators.

- **Chirp:** Zero (transform-limited). The soliton should be unchirped.

### SH pulse at input

- **Not injected.** Start with fundamental only; the SH is generated by the
  cascading process. In the large-Δk limit, the SH builds up and oscillates
  at a small amplitude. In the moderate-Δk regime, the SH grows to be a
  significant companion.

### Cell 1: Cascaded Regime — Effective Kerr Soliton (Δk·L >> 1)

Choose a temperature giving large Δk (cascaded limit). Launch a transform-
limited sech² pulse at the fundamental wavelength.

**Experiment:** Scan input pulse energy from 0.1× to 5× the estimated soliton
energy E_sol.

**Plot A:** Output pulse shape for several input energies, overlaid on a
common time axis. At E < E_sol, the pulse disperses (broadens). At E ≈ E_sol,
the pulse propagates without changing shape. At E > E_sol, the pulse narrows
(higher-order soliton behavior) or sheds radiation and settles to a
fundamental soliton.

**Plot B:** Output pulse FWHM vs. input energy. Should show a minimum (or
plateau) at the soliton energy.

**Plot C:** Temporal evolution along z — a color map of |A(z,t)|² showing
the pulse either dispersing, propagating unchanged, or breathing. This is
the money visualization for solitons.

**Plot D:** SH field amplitude along z. In the cascaded limit, it should
oscillate rapidly (period = 2·L_coh) at small amplitude, slaved to the
fundamental envelope. It should NOT grow secularly.

**Expected behavior:** The fundamental pulse behaves like a Kerr soliton.
The SH is a small parasitic ripple. The pulse energy for soliton formation
should roughly match the estimate from the effective-Kerr model.

### Cell 2: Cascaded Regime — Soliton Self-Compression

A well-known application of cascaded χ⁽²⁾: launch a pulse at N ≈ 2–5 times
the fundamental soliton power. The higher-order soliton dynamics compress the
pulse before it breaks apart.

**Experiment:** Launch at 3× soliton power.

**Plot:** Temporal evolution showing initial compression, then (if the crystal
is long enough) soliton fission — the compressed pulse breaks into a
fundamental soliton plus dispersive radiation. This is the χ⁽²⁾ analog of
supercontinuum-generating soliton fission in fibers, but mediated entirely by
cascading.

**Spectral plot:** Show the spectrum broadening during compression and the
generation of dispersive waves (resonant radiation) at specific wavelengths
set by the dispersion profile. If the broadening is significant, this
demonstrates that cascaded χ⁽²⁾ can produce supercontinuum-like spectral
broadening without any explicit χ⁽³⁾ term — connecting back to the earlier
supercontinuum discussion.

### Cell 3: Quadratic Regime — Two-Color Soliton (Δk·L ~ 1–10)

Reduce Δk by shifting temperature toward (but not reaching) the phase-matching
point. Now the SH field is non-negligible — it's a dynamically active partner.

**Experiment:** Same energy scan as Cell 1, but at moderate Δk.

**Plot A:** Output temporal profiles of BOTH fundamental and SH fields,
overlaid. At the soliton energy, both should propagate as a bound pair with
the same group velocity (despite having different free-propagation group
velocities — the nonlinear coupling locks them together). This is the
distinctive signature of a quadratic soliton.

**Plot B:** Temporal evolution along z for both fields — a two-panel color
map showing FH and SH co-propagating.

**Plot C:** Energy partition — fraction of total energy in FH vs. SH as a
function of Δk. At large Δk, almost all energy stays in FH. As Δk → 0,
the SH carries an increasing fraction. At Δk = 0, this transitions into
the SHG regime (not a soliton at all — efficient conversion).

**Expected behavior:** The two-color pulse propagates as a unit. The SH
pulse is narrower in time than the FH (a known property of quadratic solitons).
The soliton energy increases as Δk decreases (stronger coupling → more
energy needed to maintain the bound state). If you launch below soliton
energy, the fields separate — the FH disperses and the SH, now unbound,
disperses at its own (different) group velocity.

### Cell 4: Soliton Existence Map

Combine results from Cells 1–3 into a phase diagram.

**Plot:** In the (Δk, peak power) plane, color-code the output into regions:

- **Dispersive:** Pulse broadens monotonically (too little power or wrong Δk)
- **Fundamental soliton:** Pulse propagates stably
- **Higher-order / breathing:** Pulse compresses and re-expands periodically
- **SHG-dominated:** Efficient conversion to SH (near Δk = 0)

Run a coarse grid of (Δk, P₀) simulations and classify each by output
pulse width relative to input. This map shows where quadratic solitons
live in parameter space.

**This is the plot a researcher would want** to decide operating parameters.

### Cell 5: Robustness and Perturbations

Test the soliton against realistic imperfections:

**5a: Propagation loss.** Add waveguide loss (0.1–1 dB/cm) and show the
soliton adiabatically shedding energy and broadening. For small loss, the
soliton adjusts — its amplitude drops and width increases to maintain the
soliton area theorem (peak power × duration² = constant for Kerr-like
solitons). For large loss, it eventually drops below soliton threshold and
disperses.

**5b: Non-sech input.** Launch a Gaussian pulse at the soliton energy and
show that it reshapes into a sech, shedding the excess as dispersive
radiation. This demonstrates the attractor property of solitons — they
aren't fragile.

**5c: Poling disorder.** Add random fluctuations to the domain lengths
(±5%, ±10% of Λ) and check if the soliton survives. In the cascaded
limit, the soliton shouldn't care much about individual domains (it only
sees the average χ⁽²⁾). In the quadratic regime near Δk ≈ 0, it will be
more sensitive.

Summary cell comparing regimes:

## Notes for CC

1. **Dispersion is everything.** The sign and magnitude of β₂ at the
   operating wavelength completely determines whether solitons can exist
   and what they look like. If SNOW's waveguide model gives normal GVD
   everywhere from 1–2 µm, we need to either engineer a different waveguide
   geometry (thinner film, narrower width to push ZDW below 1550 nm),
   operate at a longer wavelength, or work with the self-defocusing case
   (Δk < 0, normal GVD, dark soliton). Cell 0 output determines the
   strategy for everything else.

2. **Soliton period.** The fundamental soliton has a characteristic length
   scale z₀ = T₀² / |β₂| (the soliton period is π·z₀/2). For T₀ = 100 fs
   and |β₂| = 100 fs²/mm, z₀ = 0.1 mm, so the soliton period is ~0.16 mm.
   In a 20 mm waveguide, the pulse goes through ~125 soliton periods. This
   is plenty for the soliton to establish itself, but it also means higher-
   order solitons will show many compression/expansion cycles. The z-grid
   needs to resolve these.

3. **Temporal and spectral window.** Soliton compression (Cell 2) can
   produce very short pulses (sub-50 fs) and correspondingly broad spectra.
   Make sure the grid covers enough bandwidth to capture dispersive wave
   emission without aliasing. N = 2¹² to 2¹⁴ should be sufficient (these
   are short pulses, not CW, so the grid sizing is more like the bulk OPA
   notebook than the CW OPA).

4. **Monitoring SH independently.** For Cell 3 (quadratic soliton), it's
   essential to extract and plot the SH field separately from the FH. If
   SNOW returns a combined field on the full grid, bandpass-filter the
   spectrum to separate FH and SH components for visualization. The SH
   occupies a distinct spectral window (around 775 nm for 1550 nm FH)
   so this should be straightforward.

5. **z-resolved output.** Cells 1–3 need the field at intermediate
   propagation points, not just the crystal endpoints. If SNOW's solver
   doesn't return intermediate z-slices by default, add a callback or
   intermediate save. The (z, t) color map is the single most illuminating
   visualization for soliton dynamics — without it, you just see "pulse
   in, pulse out" with no sense of what happened in between.

6. **Computational cost.** Cell 4 (existence map) requires a 2D parameter
   sweep — maybe 20 Δk values × 20 power values = 400 simulations. Each
   is a 20 mm propagation with N ~ 2¹², so ~seconds on GPU with JAX. Total
   maybe 10–30 minutes. Parallelize across the sweep if possible.

7. **Connection to earlier work.** The SHG simulation from Tutorial 5 and
   the FDTD work in the technical note modeled the same FH/SH interaction
   at perfect phase matching. This notebook explores what happens when you
   deliberately break that phase matching — the same nonlinearity, used
   differently. The technical note's numerical-dispersion Δk correction
   was actually eliminating a parasitic cascaded phase shift that was
   corrupting the SHG efficiency. Here we're embracing that phase shift
   as the desired effect. Worth noting the irony.

8. **The supercontinuum connection.** If Cell 2 (soliton self-compression)
   produces significant spectral broadening and dispersive wave emission,
   that's exactly the mechanism by which people generate octave-spanning
   spectra in TFLN without any χ⁽³⁾ — cascaded-χ⁽²⁾ soliton dynamics
   doing what Kerr solitons do in fiber. This connects directly to the
   earlier supercontinuum question: SNOW *can* model the χ⁽²⁾-mediated
   version, and this cell would demonstrate it.
